When a customer messages you on WhatsApp asking "where's my order," is a person answering, or is a
system replying automatically? The answer depends on whether your business is running the WhatsApp
Business App or the WhatsApp Business Platform (Cloud API). They share a name but were built for
different jobs — this guide explains the real difference, what the API actually enables, and its
rules, based on Meta's own documentation.

## WhatsApp Business App vs. WhatsApp Business Platform (API)

The **WhatsApp Business App** is a free app small businesses download and run from a phone. It
offers a business profile, a catalog, and basic automation like greeting messages — but it's
managed by hand, meaning someone has to open the phone and read the messages.

The **WhatsApp Business Platform**, in [Meta's own words](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/about-the-platform),
lets businesses "programmatically message and call" on WhatsApp. It isn't an app — it's an API
that plugs directly into your CRM, e-commerce system, or an AI assistant. That means a message can
be sent by a predefined rule or an AI model, not just by a human.

<Callout type="info" title="Not competitors — different scales">
  If you're a one-person business, the Business App is probably enough. Once you want to automate
  order-status notifications, appointment reminders, or dozens-to-hundreds of repetitive questions
  a day, you need the Platform (API) side.
</Callout>

| Criterion | WhatsApp Business App | WhatsApp Business Platform (API) |
|---|---|---|
| How it's used | Manual, from a phone | Integrated into systems, programmatic |
| Cost | Free | Billed per template message |
| Automation | Limited (basic greeting/away message) | Full automation, rule- or AI-based |
| Team access | Single device / limited multi-device | Unlimited system integration |
| CRM / e-commerce link | None | Direct via webhooks and API |
| Message volume | Low-to-medium, trackable by hand | High volume, many messages per second |
| Approval process | None | Requires WABA setup + template approval |
| Best fit | Solo operator / small business | Growing business, multi-channel operations |

## What the API actually delivers for a business

Strip away the technical layer and the practical value lands in five areas:

- **Automated replies.** Common questions (hours, address, delivery time) get answered instantly,
  no one has to be online.
- **Catalog and order flows.** A product catalog can be shown inside WhatsApp, and order-status
  changes ("shipped," "delivered") trigger automatic messages.
- **Appointment reminders.** As an appointment date approaches, a reminder goes out automatically —
  no one at the front desk has to call.
- **CRM integration.** Incoming and outgoing messages connect to your existing order, inventory, or
  customer system through webhooks; nothing gets copied by hand.
- **A chatbot that answers from your own data.** The assistant draws answers from your product
  catalog, FAQ, or policies (via a RAG architecture) rather than general internet knowledge, and
  hands off to a human when it isn't sure.

## Template messages and the 24-hour customer service window

<BlogFigure name="whatsapp-business-api" alt="Message lifecycle: a send that starts with an approved template, then the delivered and read states; the customer reply opens the free-form window, and when it closes you are back to templates." caption="While the window is open you can write freely; once it closes, only an approved template can start the contact again." />

WhatsApp has two message types: **free-form text** and **template messages**. If a user has
messaged you within the last 24 hours, that "customer service window" is open, and you can send
whatever free-form text you want. But once the window closes, or if you're the one initiating the
conversation, [Meta's own documentation states](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/templates/overview)
that "template messages are the only type of message that can be sent to WhatsApp users outside of
a customer service window."

Templates are submitted to Meta for approval in advance and fall into one of three categories:

1. **Marketing** — campaigns, announcements, re-engagement.
2. **Utility** — a follow-up tied to a user's action: order confirmation, appointment reminder,
   delivery update.
3. **Authentication** — one-time passwords (OTP), verification codes.

Category choice isn't cosmetic — Meta validates a template's content against its declared category,
and a mismatched category can get it rejected or reclassified later.

## Opt-in rules: who you can message, and how

This is the rule most businesses skip, and the one Meta enforces most strictly. Per the [official
opt-in documentation](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/getting-opt-in),
businesses must get explicit consent before messaging a user, and that consent must clearly state
**the business's name** and **the purpose** of the WhatsApp communication. Accepted opt-in methods
include SMS, a website form, an IVR phone flow, and an in-person or paper signature.

<Callout type="warn" title="Opt-in isn't a formality — it's an enforced rule">
  Messaging without consent can get an account restricted or suspended. Users also have the right
  to opt out of specific message categories at any time, and businesses must honor that clearly. If
  patient or customer data is involved, this needs to be planned alongside the principles in our
  [privacy policy](/en/privacy) — this article isn't legal advice, just the technical/operational
  framework.
</Callout>

## How the pricing model actually works

WhatsApp Business Platform pricing has changed in recent years and keeps evolving, so rather than
quoting a fixed number, here's how the model works. Per [Meta's official pricing
page](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/pricing), on July
1, 2025 billing moved "from conversation-based pricing to per-message pricing" — meaning you're no
longer charged for a 24-hour conversation window, but **per delivered template message**. The rate
varies by template category (marketing, utility, authentication) and the recipient's country code.

The same page states several message types are free: free-form text inside an open customer service
window, utility templates delivered within an open window, and messages sent within the first 72
hours after a user starts a conversation via an ad or page CTA. Because the pricing model itself has
shifted more than once in the last year, budgeting off last year's numbers can mislead — we always
confirm current rates directly on [Meta's pricing page](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/pricing)
as part of scoping a new project.

## Setup steps

Setting up the API isn't a "sign up and go" flow. Per [Meta's Cloud API get-started
guide](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/get-started), the
sequence looks like this:

1. **Create a Meta Developer account and a Meta App**, and add the WhatsApp product to it.
2. **Create or connect a WhatsApp Business Account (WABA)** to that app.
3. **Add and verify a phone number** — that number can no longer be used in the Business App;
   the same number can't run on both systems at once.
4. **Generate an access token** and set up a **webhook** (callback URL) for incoming messages and
   status updates.
5. **Draft the message templates you'll send and submit them for Meta's review** — approval time
   can vary depending on business verification status.
6. **Send a first test message**, then move into real integration (CRM, order system, chatbot).

Each step looks simple on its own, but decisions like which business account the WABA belongs to,
which phone number gets used, and which category a template is submitted under are hard to change
later — they need to be planned correctly from the start.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Sending bulk messages without opt-in.** The most common and most costly mistake; it can lead
   to account restriction or suspension.
2. **Choosing the wrong template category.** Submitting a reminder as "marketing" both slows down
   approval and can change the cost.
3. **Building a chatbot with no human-handoff path.** If the assistant makes up an answer to a
   question it isn't sure about, the resulting loss of trust outweighs the speed WhatsApp was
   supposed to add.
4. **Confusing the Business App number with the API number.** The same number can't be active on
   both systems — decide up front which side keeps which number.
5. **Budgeting against the old pricing model.** Because the model has changed, planning this year's
   budget off last year's figures can mislead; always confirm current rates on Meta's own page.

WhatsApp Business API isn't a "grown-up version" of the Business App — it's infrastructure built
