"Agency or freelancer" usually gets argued on price — but the real difference is about **who carries
responsibility for what**. A freelancer sells you one person's time and skill directly; an agency
sells you a process, several specialties, and an accountability for that process's outcome. Both are
legitimate models; the mistake is assuming one of them regardless of the project's size.

## What the two models actually sell

### Freelancer: one person's time and skill

Working with a freelancer, you deal with a single person. They design it, they write the code, and
often they think through content and SEO too. That means low overhead and direct communication —
there's no middleman, every question goes straight to the person doing the work. The cost is a
**capacity and continuity limit**: one person can focus on one job at a time, and can get sick, go on
holiday, or take another job.

### Agency: a coordinated process across several specialties

The agency model runs design, development, content and testing as separate specialties inside one
process. The payoff is that no stage gets bottlenecked on a single person; the cost is that
coordination itself becomes a line item — in our
[corporate website cost](/en/blog/corporate-website-cost) article we showed that only about 40% of
the budget goes directly to development, with the rest spread across discovery, design, content and
testing. That distribution is the natural result of the agency model.

## Decision matrix

Read each row against your own project. Whichever column holds the majority is your answer.

| Criterion | Favours freelancer | Favours agency |
|---|---|---|
| Project scope | Work that needs one specialty (e.g. only design or only code) | Design + development + SEO/content together |
| Budget | Limited, one-off | Medium/high, multi-stage |
| Time pressure | Fast start on a small job | Advantage of parallel work on a big job |
| Continuity needs | Low — a one-time delivery is enough | High — maintenance, support, growth plan in place |
| Accountability | Based on personal trust | Contractual, process-based, institutional backup |
| Risk tolerance | You accept dependency on one person | Team redundancy, no single point of failure |
| Brand/reputation stakes | Simple, low-risk work | Corporate identity that needs to stand out from competitors |

## The freelancer's realest risk: continuity

<Callout type="warn" title="Dependency on one person is a business risk, not a technology risk">
  A freelancer can get sick, take another job, or go silent. That's not a matter of bad faith, it's
  the natural limit of one-person capacity. What lowers the risk isn't the freelancer's skill — it's
  how the contract and the delivery process are set up.
</Callout>

Four practical steps that lower the risk:

1. **Define milestone deliveries.** Split the project into weekly or staged handoffs instead of one
   "I'll deliver when it's done" block; the source code should pass to you at every milestone.
2. **Put source code and account ownership in writing up front.** Make it explicit in the contract
   whose name the domain, hosting and code repository are registered under — detail in
   [our FAQ](/en/faq#faq-who-owns-the-code).
3. **Don't accept undocumented handoffs.** Without written setup steps and environment variables, no
   one else can pick up where a freelancer left off.
4. **Don't skip contract and confidentiality clauses** — especially on a site that handles customer
   data. Ask about [contracts and NDAs](/en/faq#faq-contract-and-nda) at the quoting stage.

These risks don't disappear in the agency model, but they get distributed: even if one person leaves,
the process and documentation stay with the team.
[Post-launch support](/en/faq#faq-after-launch-support) is also typically a standard part of the
contract.

## When a freelancer is the right choice

1. **The work is defined by a single specialty** — just a logo, just editing a landing page, just a
   bug fix.
2. **The budget is genuinely tight** and the scope is small. For a standard five-page site, the
   process overhead an agency brings can be disproportionate — detail in
   [our FAQ on small projects](/en/faq#faq-small-projects).
3. **You already have technical oversight** — if someone on your team can read code, they can review
   the freelancer's output and lower the risk themselves.
4. **It's a small, limited-scope update to an existing site.**

## When an agency is the right choice

1. **The project needs three or more specialties at once** — design, development, content, SEO/GEO
   and integration all need to run together and consistently.
2. **The margin for error has to be low.** If your corporate identity's digital presence, first
   impression and brand consistency matter, staying dependent on one person's bottleneck is risky.
3. **Post-launch growth is planned** — if a panel, multiple languages or new modules are on the
   roadmap, you need a structure with continuity. We cover this need in our
   [web application service](/en/services/web-app).
4. **Accountability matters** — if you want a contract, a process, insurance and an institutional
   point of contact.

## When neither is needed

Honestly: for some projects the right answer is neither an agency nor a freelancer. For a one-page
brochure site with fixed content and no technical integration needs, a ready-made website builder
(Wix, Squarespace and similar) is both cheaper and faster — we cover how to make that call in detail
in [website builder vs. professional website](/en/blog/website-builder-vs-professional-website). At
Piton Studios, our honest advice for this kind of project is not to hire an agency you don't need —
we're here for when your budget grows and your needs get more complex.

## The process difference: how day-to-day work changes

A difference that matters as much as price, but gets talked about less, is how the work actually
progresses.

Working with a freelancer, you have a single point of contact: whoever answers your question is the
person doing the work, no middleman. That's fast but fragile — if that person is busy or working in a
different time zone, response time stretches directly. Most freelancers serve several clients in
parallel; your project isn't their only priority on the calendar.

In the agency model, a project or account manager bridges the technical team and you. That adds a
layer — on some tasks it can feel like unnecessary friction — but its benefit is that the work doesn't
stop if one team member leaves or is out. We walked through this process step by step, from discovery
to launch, in [how a corporate web project actually runs](/en/blog/corporate-web-project-process).

<Callout type="tip" title="Test the process at the quoting stage">
  Before signing with a freelancer or an agency, test communication speed with a small question.
  Response time is a good indicator of the communication experience you'll get in the later months of
  the project.
</Callout>

## How to evaluate a portfolio and references

The most common mistake in evaluating both freelancers and agencies is judging the portfolio purely
on visual quality. The questions worth asking are different:

1. **Is this project live, and is it still running?** Ask for a real link, not a screenshot.
2. **Is it a similar size to your project?** When evaluating a corporate site, seeing only personal
   portfolio pieces isn't a strong enough signal.
3. **Can you talk to the reference directly?** One email or a short call clears up most inflated
   claims.
4. **How many people worked on the project?** Work shown in an agency's portfolio may be the team's
   output, not one individual's — that calls for a different read than evaluating a freelancer.

## What to watch for when setting up a hybrid model

For an agency-plus-freelancer hybrid to work in practice, three conditions need to be met:

- **Documentation has to be complete.** If architectural decisions, environment variables and setup
  steps aren't written down, a second person can't take over the work.
- **The boundary of responsibility has to be clear.** If it's unclear which work sits with the agency
  and which with the freelancer, responsibility becomes disputed the moment something breaks.
- **Access management has to be tidy.** Hosting, domain and code repository access need to transfer
  quickly when a person changes; this is typically something our
  [cloud ecosystem service](/en/services/cloud-ecosystem) covers.

## Five questions to ask when getting quotes

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Is the full scope included at this price? | A missing line item means an extra quote later |
| Who will own the source code and accounts? | Determines your freedom to change providers |
| Are there milestone deliveries, and how often? | Lets you catch continuity risk early |
| How many months of post-launch support are included? | Lets you plan maintenance cost up front |
| Is there a contract and a confidentiality clause? | Lowers data and business-continuity risk |

## Conclusion

The right question isn't "who's better" but **"how much risk tolerance and complexity does this
project have."** For a small, single-specialty job on a limited budget, a freelancer or website
builder is a reasonable call. For a project that needs several specialties coordinated, has a low
margin for error, and needs continuity, the agency model is the safer bet from the start.
