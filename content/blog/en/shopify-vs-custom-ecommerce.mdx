The difference between Shopify and a custom-built e-commerce platform resembles the
WordPress-vs-Next.js debate, with one twist: in e-commerce, **the flow of money** is tied directly to
the platform. Shopify gives you a ready store engine, payment infrastructure and app ecosystem; in
exchange it takes a monthly subscription plus, on most plans, a transaction percentage. A custom build
removes that percentage but requires building the store engine, inventory/order logic and integrations
from scratch.

## What the two models sell

### Shopify: a managed store engine

Shopify offers product catalogue, cart, checkout, inventory and order management as a ready system.
Setup takes days, security and infrastructure maintenance are Shopify's responsibility, and thousands
of ready-made apps let you add shipping, accounting and marketing integrations. The cost is that your
store stays dependent on Shopify's data model and app ecosystem.

### Custom build: store logic built from scratch

A custom-built e-commerce site tailors your product model, checkout flow and integrations exactly to
your business. If you have an ERP or a custom pricing logic, you can implement it without being
limited to whatever the platform allows. The cost is that the store engine (cart, inventory, payment
security) has to be built from scratch and maintained — a need we cover in our
[web application service](/en/services/web-app).

## Pricing: subscription plus a transaction percentage

Per [Shopify's official pricing page](https://www.shopify.com/pricing) (as of September 2026, in US
dollars, excluding current promotions), the plan structure looks like this:

| Plan | Monthly, on annual billing | Third-party payment provider transaction fee |
|---|---|---|
| Basic | $19 | 2% |
| Grow | $54 | 1% |
| Advanced | $299 | 0.6% |
| Plus | $2,300 and up | 0.2% |

<Callout type="info" title="The transaction fee only applies with a third-party payment provider">
  Stores using Shopify Payments don't pay this extra fee, though standard card processing costs always
  apply. Stores in Türkiye using a local payment provider (iyzico, PayTR and similar) mostly fall into
  the third-party category — read the table against your own payment setup accordingly.
</Callout>

This is the core thing that sets Shopify apart from other platform decisions: with WordPress or Wix
you pay a fixed subscription, but on Shopify (for stores using a third-party payment provider), cost
grows together with your revenue. As revenue grows, that percentage can turn into a significant
absolute line item; the only way to see the break-even point is to run the numbers against your own
revenue projection.

## The app ecosystem: a hidden subscription layer

Shopify's biggest strength is its app store — for most needs like shipping calculation, loyalty
programmes, email marketing and accounting integration, a ready-made app exists. That speeds things
up, but it usually brings its own subscription too: on top of the platform subscription, a store adds
several more app subscriptions as it grows. This is the equivalent of plugin overhead on WordPress —
in [Next.js or WordPress](/en/blog/nextjs-vs-wordpress) we showed that plugin maintenance is the
deciding line item in a three-year total; on Shopify that line item isn't plugin maintenance, it's
accumulated app subscriptions.

On a custom build, the same functions (shipping calculation, email integration) are built once and
don't create a recurring subscription fee — the cost is a longer development and initial setup time.

## A middle path: headless/hybrid architecture

You don't have to make a sharp either/or choice. A growing third model keeps Shopify purely as the
product, inventory and payment engine in the background, and builds the storefront (the part customers
see and shop on) as a custom frontend — this is called headless or hybrid e-commerce. This approach
keeps Shopify's transaction security and inventory management while giving full performance and design
control on the frontend. The cost is the added architectural complexity of running two systems
(Shopify backend plus custom frontend) together. This model can be a meaningful middle ground,
especially for brands with high traffic that want a non-standard shopping experience.

## Don't reduce the evaluation to a single metric

The most common mistake people arguing against Shopify make is deciding purely on the transaction
percentage. That fee matters, but on its own it's an incomplete picture — weigh these four cost items
together:

1. **Platform subscription** — fixed, predictable.
2. **Transaction fee** (if using a third-party payment provider) — grows with revenue.
3. **App subscriptions** — accumulate as the store grows, and often go unnoticed.
4. **Development and maintenance** — upfront on a custom build; on Shopify it shows up when theme/app
   customisation is needed.

Whether a store says "Shopify is cheaper" or "custom is cheaper" depends on how these four items are
weighted for it. A store running a standard catalogue with few apps and low-to-medium revenue keeps
Shopify's total cost low. Once many apps, custom integrations and high revenue come together, the
picture changes quickly.

## Decision matrix

| Criterion | Favours Shopify | Favours custom build |
|---|---|---|
| Product count | Low-medium, standard category structure | High, custom variant/pricing logic |
| Revenue size | Low-medium (transaction fee stays small) | High (fee grows in absolute terms) |
| Integrations | Ready-made app exists (shipping, accounting) | Custom ERP, CRM, B2B pricing |
| Technical capacity | Not required, platform manages it | Needs a developer or agency |
| Launch speed | Live within days | Takes weeks |
| Long-term control | Platform-bound data model | Full control, portable |
| SEO/performance customisation | Within theme and app limits | Unlimited, managed via a performance budget |

## When Shopify is the right choice

1. **Launching fast is the priority** — the catalogue is ready and you need to start selling within
   days.
2. **Revenue is still low-to-medium.** The transaction fee isn't a meaningful cost item at small
   amounts.
3. **Needs are standard** — shipping, accounting and marketing integrations can be met with ready-made
   apps.
4. **There's no technical maintenance capacity** — if you don't want to deal with security and
   infrastructure management.

## When to move to a custom build

1. **The transaction fee turns into a meaningful absolute line item as revenue grows.** Calculate the
   break-even point with your own projection — the chart above is only a scenario.
2. **You need an ERP, custom B2B pricing, or complex variant logic.** Needs like these get fragile
   when forced onto ready-made apps.
3. **Full control over data and infrastructure matters** — if platform portability is part of your
   long-term strategy.
4. **Performance and SEO are a target that will be written into a contract.** If you want a measured
   performance budget without theme and app limits, a custom build is more predictable.

## Which size store falls on which side

Giving a hard product-count threshold would be misleading, because what decides this isn't catalogue
size, it's business-model complexity. Still, the pattern we see in practice: a store with hundreds of
standard products, one currency, selling into one market runs comfortably on Shopify. A store with
multiple currencies, dealer-specific pricing, inventory sync across a physical store network, or a B2B
wholesale flow finds its maintenance getting complicated quickly when it forces those needs onto apps.
At that point the question stops being "Shopify or custom" and becomes "which infrastructure carries
this business logic with less friction."

If your store is already live and the real problem is conversion, not traffic, look at the funnel
analysis in [ecommerce conversion rate optimization](/en/blog/ecommerce-conversion-rate-optimization)
before switching platforms — most of the time the real point of loss is friction in checkout, not the
platform.

## Data portability and lock-in

On Shopify, product, order and customer data is stored according to the platform's data model. Standard
export tools exist, but the data model doesn't map one-to-one between platforms — custom fields,
discount rules and loyalty data in particular can be lost or need rebuilding in a migration. That's not
a malicious restriction, it's the natural result of any managed platform; the same happens moving from
WooCommerce to Shopify too.

On a custom build, you define the data model yourself, so portability is in your hands from the start
— but the cost of that control is designing the data model, backups and security from scratch. We
cover how we make that responsibility manageable in our
[cloud ecosystem service](/en/services/cloud-infrastructure).

## Conclusion

The difference between Shopify and a custom build isn't a quality ranking, it's a question of **how
cost grows**: on Shopify, cost grows with your revenue; on a custom build, development cost is upfront
and maintenance stays low after. For a store with few products, wanting to launch fast, with revenue
still at a low-to-medium level, Shopify is an honest choice. As revenue grows, integrations get more
complex, or full control is required, a custom build pays off from the start.
