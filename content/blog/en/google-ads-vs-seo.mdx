This question is usually asked as "which one is better" — but the two operate under entirely
different physical rules. Google Ads is a **rental model** — you're visible as long as you pay, and
you disappear the moment you stop. SEO is a **compounding asset** — it starts slow, but the gains
stick. We already gave the short answer in [our FAQ page](/en/faq#faq-google-ads-vs-seo); this
article goes deeper into how the two channels actually work, their official thresholds, and how to
split your budget between them.

## How Google Ads works: a system that's hungry for data

Google Ads' Smart Bidding strategies (Target CPA, Target ROAS, Maximize Conversions) are automated
systems that learn from historical conversion data. According to
[Google Ads Help's Target CPA page](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6268632?hl=en), a
reliable performance evaluation needs **at least 30 conversions accumulated over the last 30 days**.
The [Target ROAS page](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6268637?hl=en) breaks the
activation threshold down by campaign type:

| Campaign type | Activation threshold (Google Ads Help) |
|---|---|
| Search / Shopping | At least 15 conversions in the last 30 days |
| Display | At least 15 conversions combined across all campaigns (with a valid value) |
| Demand Gen | At least 50 conversions in the last 35 days |
| Hotel | At least 50 conversions per campaign per week |

## Cost structure: neither one is "free"

Google Ads' cost is visible: you pay per click, and impressions stop once the budget runs out. SEO's
cost is less visible but just as real — content production, technical work and ongoing maintenance
take time and effort; "SEO is free" only ever meant there's no pay-per-click charge, not that the
work costs nothing. The real difference is where the spend goes:

- **With Google Ads**, most of the budget goes to the platform (cost per click), with the rest going
  to management and creative production.
- **With SEO**, most of the budget goes to content production, technical fixes and editorial work —
  there's no direct payment to a platform.

This also makes the cash-flow risk of the two channels different: a bad month on Ads is noticed
immediately and spend can be cut; a bad quarter of SEO work only shows up in traffic data months
later — which makes regular measurement of SEO work even more critical.

## How SEO works: a system that starts slow and compounds

SEO has no threshold, but its timeline looks uncertain. According to
[Google's own SEO Starter Guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/seo-starter-guide):

> "Some changes might take effect in a few hours, others could take several months. In general,
> you likely want to wait a few weeks to assess whether your work had beneficial effects in
> Google Search results."

It doesn't give a firm date because it can't — the outcome depends on the site's current state, how
intense the competition is, and content quality. But this distinction is certain: **with Google
Ads, results start the moment the campaign launches; with SEO, results wait on content and technical
changes being indexed and evaluated.**

<Callout type="key" title="What compounding actually means">
  A page you publish today with SEO can still bring traffic a year from now — and often more, as it
  accumulates age and links. With Google Ads, last month's ad spend contributes nothing to this
  month's traffic. That's the core difference that gets overlooked when comparing the long-term
  return of the two channels.
</Callout>

## Decision matrix

Read each row against your own project. Whichever column holds the majority is your answer.

| Criterion | Favours Google Ads | Favours SEO & GEO |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Traffic as soon as the campaign launches | Impact over weeks to months |
| When paused | Traffic resets instantly | Gains decline gradually |
| Measurability | Immediate, granular | Delayed, multi-variable |
| Testing | A new offer/campaign is validated fast | A new positioning takes longer to see results |
| Budget risk | Spending stops, risk resets — but so do gains | Upfront investment pays back over a longer horizon |
| Competitive keywords | Competed for on cost per click | Competed for on time and content quality |
| Brand trust | An "Ad" label reduces trust for some users | Organic results are perceived as more credible |

## When to lean into Google Ads

1. **You need to validate a new offer or market fast.** A campaign can be set up and deliver first
   data within days.
2. **You're running a seasonal or limited-time campaign.** SEO's timeline doesn't fit that window.
3. **You already have enough traffic volume to generate 15-30+ conversions a month.** Otherwise the
   learning phase stays inefficient and your budget burns while waiting for the campaign to
   self-optimise.
4. **You want visibility on competitors' brand name searches.** That's a position almost impossible
   to win organically.

## When to lean into SEO & GEO

1. **Your budget needs to be fixed and sustainable.** If you want an asset that doesn't reset when
   spending stops, this is the right channel.
2. **You're targeting long-term, high repeat-search-volume keywords.** SEO's compounding effect
   kicks in on keywords whose return grows over time.
3. **You also want visibility in AI-powered search results.** We covered this in depth in our
   [search visibility in the AI era article](/en/blog/search-visibility-in-the-ai-era-seo-to-geo)
   under the shift from SEO to GEO.
4. **Brand credibility matters.** Organic results, carrying no ad label, are perceived as more
   trustworthy in some categories.

## The measurement and data-ownership difference

With Google Ads, measurement is immediate and granular — you can see within the same day which
keyword, which ad copy and which hour drove conversions. That data is valuable but
platform-dependent: close your account and the accumulated learning resets with it.

With SEO, measurement is more delayed and multi-variable — it takes time to separate whether a
ranking change came from a content update, a competitor's move, or a Google algorithm update. In
return, the content you produce and the technical structure you build stay with you; even if you
change agency or provider, the accumulated asset stays on your site.

## Common mistakes

1. **Turning on Google Ads and killing it a week later because "it isn't working."** Decisions made
   before the learning phase completes never give the system a chance to learn.
2. **Starting SEO and expecting results a month later.** By Google's own account, that timeline
   isn't realistic; judging results before a few weeks have passed gives a misleading picture.
3. **Competing with yourself on the same keyword in both channels.** Paying for an ad on a keyword
   where you're already ranking #1 organically is often just buying the same click twice — that
   budget should go toward keywords you don't already own organically.
4. **Treating SEO as a one-and-done job.** Content and technical structure need maintenance; an SEO
   effort left unattended gradually cedes ground to competitors.

## Which business model leans which way

Industry alone isn't decisive, but some business models show a clear tendency:

| Business model | Tendency | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Urgent services (locksmith, plumber, tow truck) | Google Ads-weighted | The moment of search is the moment of need; users don't wait, they call whoever they see first |
| High-consideration B2B (software, consulting) | SEO & GEO-weighted | The decision process is long; content and authority build trust |
| Seasonal campaigns (holidays, sale periods) | Google Ads-weighted | SEO's timeline doesn't fit inside the campaign window |
| Local business (restaurant, clinic) | Both together | Google Ads gives fast visibility, SEO & Maps deliver lasting local traffic |
| Content/media sites | SEO & GEO-weighted | The revenue model already relies on organic traffic; ad cost erodes the margin |

This table isn't a rule, it's a starting point. The final decision depends on how intense the
competition is, existing brand awareness, and how ready the conversion funnel is — which is why in

## Running both together: the recommended model

The healthiest approach is generally not "either/or" but splitting by time horizon:

| Stage | Google Ads' role | SEO & GEO's role |
|---|---|---|
| Months 0-3 | Validates demand fast, produces first data | Technical foundation and content infrastructure are built |
| Months 3-9 | Proven campaigns are scaled | First rankings and traffic start to appear |
| Month 9+ | Ad budget is pulled from keywords where SEO has grown, and redirected to new areas | Organic traffic share grows, acquisition cost falls |

In this model, the early demand signal from Google Ads also informs which keywords SEO should
prioritise — campaign data shows you exactly which search term actually drives conversions.

## Conclusion

Google Ads and SEO aren't rivals — they're two channels that operate on different time horizons. If
you want fast, measurable results and your conversion volume can reach Google's recommended
thresholds (15 for Search/Shopping, 30 conversions/month for reliable evaluation), Ads is the right
starting point. If you want to build a lasting asset on a fixed budget, prioritise SEO & GEO. For
most businesses, the right answer is running both together, on different timelines.

The real question when deciding isn't "which is better": can your conversion volume meet Google's
learning thresholds, how patient can your budget be, and do you need traffic that resets when ads
stop or an asset that grows gradually. For most businesses, the answer is both, on different time
horizons.
