Since 2 August 2026, anyone in the EU talking to an AI has to be told so: it is a legal requirement,
not a design choice. The transparency rules of the EU AI Act took effect, and the last-minute Digital
Omnibus package left them untouched while postponing the rules for high-risk systems. We gave the
short answer on what a website chatbot can do [in our FAQ](/en/faq#faq-chatbot-on-site); this post
explains what the new rules cover, when they apply to a business outside the EU that sells to or
serves customers in the EU, and what needs to change in practice.

## What took effect on 2 August 2026

The EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) applies in stages. 2 August 2026 was set as the start date for
the broadest package of obligations. After a political agreement in May 2026, the
[Digital Omnibus was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202601744)
and [entered into force on 27 July](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/07/27/the-digital-omnibus-on-ai-enters-into-force-today-102nedo).
The package pushed some dates back but left the transparency rules in place.

| Obligation | Original date | Current date |
|---|---|---|
| Article 50 transparency rules (chatbot disclosure, deepfake labels) | 2 August 2026 | 2 August 2026 (unchanged) |
| Machine-readable marking of AI content, systems on the market before 2 August 2026 | 2 August 2026 | 2 December 2026 |
| Annex III high-risk systems (recruitment, credit scoring, education, etc.) | 2 August 2026 | 2 December 2027 |
| Annex I high-risk systems (AI embedded in regulated products) | 2 August 2027 | 2 August 2028 |

## When it applies to a business in Türkiye

The Act does not only apply to companies established in the EU. [Under Article 2](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/2/),
it also covers providers and deployers established outside the EU **when the output of the AI system
is used in the EU**. As [Jones Walker's analysis](https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/ai-law-blog/yes-august-2-still-matters-the-eu-approved-a-high-risk-ai-delay-but-most-trans.html)
stresses, a website merely being accessible from the EU is not a trigger on its own; what matters is
the business's role and the specific facts.

In practice, ask yourself the question in these scenarios:

- An e-commerce site selling to customers in the EU has an AI chat assistant.
- As a hotel, clinic or tour operator, you answer guests from the EU through a multilingual chatbot.
- You use realistic AI images or video in ads and campaigns aimed at the EU market.
- You offer an AI product you built yourself to customers in the EU.

## Provider or deployer: work out your role

The Act distributes obligations by role. The same business can be a provider for one system and a
deployer for another.

| Role | Who | Main obligation under Article 50 |
|---|---|---|
| Provider | Develops the AI system or places it on the market under its own name | Design the chatbot so it is clear it is AI; mark generated content in a machine-readable way |
| Deployer | A business using the system in its own operations | Label deepfake images, audio and video; disclose AI text published in the public interest; inform people if emotion recognition is used |

A business embedding an off-the-shelf chat tool on its site is usually a deployer; but if it offers
the tool to customers under its own brand or builds it from scratch, provider obligations come into
play too. Pinning down your role decides which rows of the list below are yours.

## The four obligations in Article 50

[Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/) covers four separate situations. The
common rule across all of them is the same: information must be given **clearly, distinguishably and
at the latest at the first interaction or exposure**, and must meet accessibility requirements.

| Situation | Who is obliged | What to do | Exception |
|---|---|---|---|
| Systems interacting directly with people (chatbots, voice assistants) | Provider | The user must know they are interacting with an AI | Where it is already obvious from context |
| Systems generating synthetic audio, images, video or text | Provider | Output must be marked in a machine-readable way | Tools that only assist with standard editing |
| Emotion recognition and biometric categorisation | Deployer | People exposed must be informed | Lawful law-enforcement uses |
| Deepfake images, audio, video and AI text published in the public interest | Deployer | State that the content is AI-generated | Limited notice for artistic, satirical and fictional works; text under human editorial control |

## What to do for your website chatbot in practice

Chatbot disclosure is the most concrete and easiest part of the rules to implement. The goal is for
the user to understand from the very first message that they are not talking to a human.

- **Opening message:** say that the assistant is AI in the first line shown when the chat window
  opens. This should be part of the first message, not a footnote that scrolls away.
- **Name and avatar:** do not give the assistant a real employee's name and photo; use a name or badge
  that makes clear it is AI.
- **Handover to a human:** when the conversation passes to a customer agent, say so clearly as well,
  so the user knows which message came from whom.
- **All channels:** the rule is not limited to the window on your site; an automated assistant
  running on WhatsApp must give the same notice if it serves users in the EU. For the WhatsApp setup,
  see our [WhatsApp Business API guide](/en/blog/what-is-whatsapp-business-api).
- **Accessibility:** the notice must be readable by screen readers and must not rely on an icon or a
  colour alone.

<Callout type="tip" title="Disclosure does not reduce trust; it prevents false expectations">
  When users know from the start that they are talking to an AI, they accept a handover to a human
  more easily when the assistant cannot help. The best setup turns the notice into a short welcome
  line saying what the assistant can do and when it will connect them to a person.
</Callout>

## AI-generated images, video and text

On the content side, the rules are narrower than most businesses assume, but clearer.

- **Realistic images, audio and video:** content resembling real people, places, objects or events
  that could be perceived as authentic counts as a deepfake and must be disclosed as AI-generated. An
  AI spokesperson resembling a real person in a campaign, or a generated photo that looks like a real
  venue, falls into this group.
- **Evidently artistic or fictional works:** the notice is limited to indicating that such content
  exists, in a way that does not hamper the display of the work.
- **Text:** the obligation only applies to text published to inform the public on matters of public
  interest. Product descriptions, campaign copy and corporate blog posts fall outside this general
  labelling duty; and for public-interest text, an exception applies when there is human review and
  editorial responsibility.

<Callout type="warn" title="The tool provider's marking does not replace your disclosure">
  The machine-readable mark an image tool adds to its output is the provider's obligation. The visible
  disclosure duty of the business publishing that content as a deepfake is separate and its own. Steps
  such as cropping or re-saving an image can also strip these marks.
</Callout>

## Where AI is used: Türkiye and the EU

Usage data helps show how many businesses the rules concern.
[According to Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Use_of_artificial_intelligence_in_enterprises),
19.95% of EU enterprises with 10 or more employees used at least one AI technology in 2025, up from
roughly 13.5% a year earlier. The most common use was text analysis (11.75%), followed by generating
images, video and audio (9.55%) and natural language generation and speech synthesis (8.76%).

According to TurkStat's first [AI Statistics 2025](https://www.dha.com.tr/teknoloji/tuik-yapay-zeka-teknolojileri-kullanan-girisim-orani-artti-2730034)
release, the share in Türkiye is 7.5%. The gap shows at every size.

## Checklist

1. **Take an inventory.** List every place you use AI: on the site, on WhatsApp, in email and in ads.
2. **Mark the EU link.** Identify which of these uses reach customers or users in the EU.
3. **Write down your role.** For each system, note whether you are the provider or the deployer.
4. **Update your chatbots.** Add an AI notice to the opening message, mark handovers to a human, and
   remove names and avatars that imitate a real person.
5. **Set image rules.** Write a labelling standard the team uses for realistic AI images and video.
6. **Ask your suppliers.** Ask whether the AI tools you use mark output in a machine-readable way, and
   about the 2 December 2026 timeline for legacy systems.
7. **Keep a record.** Log the changes you made and why, with dates; it is the first place to look when
   a question comes in.

## Conclusion

The part of the EU AI Act that concerns the widest audience is not the high-risk rules but the
transparency rules in force since 2 August 2026. For a business in Türkiye serving customers in the
EU, this mostly means something simple: the chatbot introduces itself in its first message, realistic
AI images are labelled, and who carries which obligation is written down. The changes are small, but
the risk ceiling when they are skipped is high.
